Six roads for sync & multiplayer

Multiplayer and privacy pull against each other, but not as hard as it first looks. Six coherent destinations, what each buys, and what each costs. Most of them compose — only two are genuinely exclusive.

The one real fork

Custody. Roads 1, 2, 5 and (local) 6 mean you never hold readable copies of anyone's work. Roads 3 and 4 mean you do.

Everything else is sequencing — you can add publishing later, add agents later, add sharing later. Custody is the decision that's expensive to reverse, because every feature you build on top of a readable server (web rendering, thumbnails, server-side screenshots, search, moderation) stops working the day you encrypt.

The corollary nobody likes: a server that can render your canvas is a server that can read it. Browser-based viewing and E2EE are not simultaneously free — Road 5 buys them back only by putting a key in the viewer's URL fragment.

What composes

1 → 2 1 → 3 2 → 5 6 → anything

Road 1 is where you are. Road 6 sits underneath all of them — a local agent needs no server at all. Roads 2 and 5 are the same product at different maturity, since private device sync is just multiplayer with one participant.

Road 4 is the one that closes doors. Once real users' work is on a readable server, moving to 5 is a migration, a re-architecture, and a promise you have to walk back.

Prior art worth reading: Ink & Switch's Keyhive (capability-based access control for local-first data, with a sync protocol the server can't decrypt — early preview, not production), the original local-first essay, and Obsidian Sync, which ships end-to-end encrypted shared vaults today and is the clearest existence proof that Road 5 is a business, not a research project.
